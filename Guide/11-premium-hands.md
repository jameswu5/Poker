# Chapter 11 — Premium Pairs & AK/AQ

Part IV is the heart of what you asked for: **how to play each type of hand**. We start at the top — the big pairs (**AA, KK, QQ**) and the premium broadways (**AK, AQ**). These are the hands you make the most money with, which is exactly why they're so often misplayed: people either get them all-in too eagerly into ranges that crush them, or get so attached they can't fold when clearly beaten. The skill with premium hands is **extracting maximum value while folding when the board and action scream that you're now second-best.** This chapter walks each hand through preflop and all three postflop streets.

Throughout, remember the framing from Part I: think in **ranges** (Ch. 3), use **board texture** (Ch. 8), and let **the action narrow villain's range** (Ch. 23 preview) rather than playing your two cards in a vacuum.

---

## 11.1 Pocket Aces (AA)

The best starting hand — but its value is concentrated **preflop and on safe boards**, and it's a *one-pair hand* postflop that can and does get beaten.

**Preflop:** always raising/re-raising. Open it, 3-bet it, 4-bet it, 5-bet jam it (Ch. 4–6). The one nuance: don't *over*-slowplay it preflop hoping to trap — at 100bb in 6-max, building the pot with AA is almost always better than getting cute, because you want money in while you're a huge favorite. Occasionally flatting a 3-bet with AA to trap (in position, vs. an aggressive 4-bettor) is fine, but raising is the default.

**Postflop — the core principle:** AA is an **overpair**, and overpairs are strong but vulnerable. Bet for value and protection, but **respect the board and the action**:
- On **dry boards** (K-7-2, Q-8-3): bet for value across streets; AA is almost always best and you can get three streets of value from worse pairs and draws. Size to charge the limited draws.
- On **wet/dynamic boards** (9-8-6, J-T-7 two-tone): bet **bigger for protection** (charge the many draws), but be ready to **slow down** if a scare card hits and the action gets heavy. An overpair on a coordinated board can be drawing slim against sets, two pairs, and made straights/flushes.
- **When to fold AA:** when the board is wet, you've put in significant action, and villain's range (given the line) is now full of hands that beat you — e.g., you bet, get raised, and barreled on a 9-8-6-5-7 runout by a tight player. *Yes, you can fold aces.* The players who can't are the ones who stack off drawing nearly dead. AA is a hand, not a religion.

> **The AA reality check.** AA wins a huge amount preflop and on dry flops, but it's *one pair*. The money you lose with AA comes from refusing to fold it when an obviously connected board and heavy, credible aggression have turned it into a bluff-catcher (or worse). Extract relentlessly when you're ahead; release when the range math says you're beaten.

## 11.2 Pocket Kings (KK)

Plays almost identically to AA, with **one extra worry: the ace on the flop.**

**Preflop:** raise/3-bet/4-bet/5-bet jam, same as AA. Against a tight player's 4-bet or 5-bet jam, KK is still a stack-off (you're ahead of their range unless they only do it with AA) — but note KK's nightmare is running into exactly AA. At 100bb, jam KK into 4-bets; against a known nit who only 4-bets AA, you have a rare, disciplined fold, but default to getting it in.

**Postflop:**
- **No ace on board:** play it like AA — bet for value and protection, sized to texture.
- **Ace on the flop (A-x-x):** now KK is just **second pair to any ace**, and many opponents have an ace (especially out of the blinds or in flatted ranges). Proceed with **caution**: a single c-bet for protection/value is fine, but **don't stack off** against meaningful resistance — you're often beaten by Ax. Treat ace-high flops as a "pot control / bet-fold" texture for KK, not a "get it in" texture.

The KK skill is **not overplaying it on ace-high boards** while still extracting full value when no ace comes.

## 11.3 Pocket Queens (QQ)

A premium hand, but the **first big pair that's genuinely tricky**, because **two overcards (A and K) can come**, and against tight ranges QQ is often the hand that's "just behind" in big preflop pots.

**Preflop:** open/3-bet for value. Facing 4-bets and 5-bets, QQ is the classic **opponent-dependent stack-off**: against loose, aggressive 4-bettors, get it in (you're ahead of their range); against tight players who only escalate with QQ+/AK, QQ is often **dominated** by their value (AA/KK) and barely flipping with AK — so **flatting or folding** to a tight 4-bet can be correct. Know your opponent.

**Postflop:**
- **No A or K on board:** QQ is an overpair — play it like KK/AA, betting for value and protection.
- **A or K on the flop:** like KK on an ace board, QQ becomes vulnerable; pot-control and bet-fold rather than stacking off into the many Ax/Kx hands.
- QQ benefits the most from the Chapter 8 habit of asking *whose range does this board favor?* — it's strong enough to bet but vulnerable enough that you must release on the wrong runouts.

## 11.4 Ace-King (AK) — "Big Slick"

The most misunderstood premium hand. AK is a **drawing hand preflop** (it's ace-high until it pairs), but a *very strong* one because of its equity, blockers, and the strength of the hands it makes.

**Preflop:** a premium. Open, 3-bet (value), and **5-bet jam** it — AK is a stack-off against most 4-betting ranges because of fold equity plus its raw equity when called (it's never worse than a coinflip vs. pairs and dominates other ace/king hands). **AKs > AKo**, but both are top-tier. Note AK's value as a **blocker**: holding the A and K removes AA, KK, and AK combos from villain's value range, which is why it's such a good 3-bet/4-bet/5-bet hand.

**Postflop:**
- **You pair (top pair top kicker):** AK makes the best top pair possible — bet for value across streets, but it's still **one pair**, so on wet boards or against heavy multi-street aggression, be willing to pot-control or fold (same logic as AA/KK).
- **You miss (ace-high):** AK is the best **semi-bluff / barreling** hand in the game when you whiff. It has **overcard equity** (six outs to top pair), **blockers** to villain's strong hands, and **backdoor draws** often. C-betting and even double-barreling AK as a bluff is highly effective — you have fold equity *and* outs, and the times you're called you can still improve. This is the model "give up later if needed, but apply pressure with equity + blockers" hand.
- **Don't overvalue a missed AK at showdown:** ace-high sometimes wins, but AK that bricks out is usually a **give-up or a bluff**, not a bluff-catcher. Use its blocker/equity to bet, or check and fold — don't talk yourself into hero-calling with ace-high just because it's "Big Slick."

## 11.5 Ace-Queen (AQ) — Strong but Dominated-Prone

AQ is a strong broadway hand, but it lives one tier below AK and carries real **domination risk** — it's beaten by AK and AA/QQ/KK, the exact hands that love big preflop pots.

**Preflop:** open and 3-bet (AQs more liberally than AQo). It's a **value 3-bet** against wide opens, but against tight ranges and especially **facing 4-bets**, AQ is often **dominated** (crushed by AK and the big pairs) — so vs. tight 4-bettors, **fold** rather than stack off. AQ is the hand that teaches the lesson: *premium-looking is not the same as happy-to-get-it-all-in.*

**Postflop:**
- **Top pair (AQ on an A-high or Q-high board):** good top pair, bet for value — but with a **kicker that can be outkicked** (AK beats your A-Q on an ace board), so on ace-high boards against heavy action, AQ is often a **bet-fold / pot-control** hand, not a stack-off. On queen-high boards it's stronger (top pair good kicker, fewer hands beat you).
- **Missed:** like AK, AQ has overcard equity and blockers, so it makes a fine **semi-bluff/barrel** hand when you whiff, though slightly weaker than AK (your Q blocks less of villain's value than a K would). Bet with the equity+blockers, or give up — don't hero-call ace-high.

## 11.6 Cross-Cutting Skills for Premium Hands

A few principles apply to *all* the hands in this chapter:

**1. Slowplaying is usually a mistake.** With AA/KK and strong made hands, **building the pot** (betting/raising) almost always beats trying to trap, because (a) you deny equity to drawing hands, and (b) you maximize value while ahead. Slowplay only in narrow spots: a totally dry board where no worse hand can call a bet (so you check to let them catch up or bluff), or against a hyper-aggressive opponent who'll bet for you. Default: **bet your big hands.**

**2. The "one pair" humility.** AA/KK/QQ and paired AK/AQ are, postflop, *one-pair hands*. One pair is strong on dry static boards and **fragile on wet dynamic boards and against multi-street aggression**. The biggest premium-hand leak is **stacking off one pair on coordinated boards** against ranges that have moved past you.

**3. Multiway, tighten up.** All these hands lose relative value multiway (Ch. 27) — overpairs and top pairs are far less likely to be best against three opponents. Bet for value/protection, but be much more willing to pot-control and fold; don't play a 100bb stack-off pot with one pair multiway without a very good reason.

**4. Blockers make AK/AQ great aggressors.** When AK/AQ miss, their **blocker value + overcard equity** make them premier semi-bluff/barreling hands. You don't need to pair to win with them — you need to apply pressure with hands that block villain's continues and can still improve.

## 11.7 The Exploitative Layer

- **vs. calling stations:** value-bet your overpairs and top pairs **bigger and thinner**, across more streets; **stop barreling AK/AQ as bluffs** when you miss (they don't fold). Pure value extraction.
- **vs. nits / tight players:** **fold QQ/AQ (and sometimes AK) to their 4-bets and heavy aggression** — when they commit, they have it. Don't pay off their value with second-best premiums.
- **vs. maniacs / over-aggressors:** **stack off wider** (KK/QQ/AK into their 4-bets), let them bluff into your overpairs (check-call/induce), and don't fold one pair to a single scary bet from someone who's always firing.
- **vs. fit-or-fold opponents:** barrel your missed AK/AQ relentlessly; their over-folding makes the semi-bluff print even without improvement.

> **The leak this chapter fixes.** Two opposite leaks: **over-attachment** (stacking off AA/KK/QQ/AQ on wet boards or into tight ranges that have you crushed) and **under-extraction** (slowplaying big hands and letting drawing hands catch up cheaply). The fix is the same idea from both sides: **bet relentlessly when the range math says you're ahead, and release without ego when it says you're behind.**

---

## 11.8 Chapter Summary

- **AA/KK/QQ** are premium **overpairs**: bet for value and protection, **size up on wet boards** to charge draws, and **fold without ego** when a coordinated board + heavy credible action shows you're beaten. They're strong *one-pair* hands, not invincible.
- **KK** fears the **ace on board** (becomes second pair — pot-control, don't stack off); **QQ** fears **A and K** and is the classic **opponent-dependent 4-bet stack-off** (get it in vs. loose, fold vs. tight).
- **AK** is a premium **drawing hand**: 5-bet jam it, make the best top pair, and when it **misses, it's the best semi-bluff/barrel hand** (overcard equity + blockers). Don't hero-call ace-high.
- **AQ** is strong but **domination-prone**: value-3-bet vs. wide ranges, but **fold to tight 4-bets** (crushed by AK/big pairs); a bet-fold/pot-control top pair, and a decent (slightly weaker) semi-bluff when missed.
- Cross-cutting: **don't slowplay** (build pots), respect the **"one pair" fragility**, **tighten multiway**, and use **AK/AQ blockers** to attack when you whiff.
- **Exploit:** value-bet huge and stop bluffing vs. stations; fold premiums to nits' aggression; stack off wide and induce vs. maniacs; barrel missed broadways vs. fit-or-fold players.
