# Chapter 12 — Medium & Small Pairs

Pocket pairs below QQ — call it **JJ down to 22** — are a different animal from the premiums. Their value comes in two very different modes: as **made hands** (an overpair or a decent pair when the board cooperates) and as **set-mining hands** (small pairs hoping to flop a set and stack someone). Playing them well means knowing *which mode you're in* on every board, getting the **set-mining math** right preflop, and resisting the urge to overplay a pair that's frequently an underdog. This chapter covers JJ–TT (the "medium" pairs that are usually overpairs or close), 99–66 (the "middle" pairs that straddle both modes), and 55–22 (the "small" pairs that are primarily set-miners).

---

## 12.1 The Two Modes of a Pocket Pair

Every pocket pair postflop is in one of two modes, decided by the board:

1. **Overpair / made-hand mode:** the board is all undercards (e.g., you hold 99 on 7-4-2). Now your pair is an **overpair** — a strong but vulnerable one-pair hand, played much like a small version of the AA/KK logic from Chapter 11: bet for value/protection, but respect aggression and overcards.
2. **Underpair / set-or-give-up mode:** an overcard (or several) is on the board (e.g., 99 on K-J-5). Now your pair is **underneath** likely top pairs — it's a marginal bluff-catcher or a fold, unless you flopped a **set** (three of a kind using your pair). Most of the time small and middle pairs are in this mode, which is why **set-mining** is their primary value source.

The smaller your pair, the more often you're in mode 2. A pair of deuces is almost never an overpair; a pair of jacks frequently is. This sliding scale drives everything below.

---

## 12.2 Set-Mining: The Math

**Set-mining** = calling a preflop raise with a small/medium pair primarily to **flop a set** (you'll flop a set or better about **11.8%** of the time — roughly **1 in 8.5**). When you hit, you have a disguised monster that wins big pots; when you miss, you usually fold cheaply. The question is whether the *implied odds* justify the call.

**The math (the "set-mining rule"):**
- You flop a set ~**1 in 8.5**, so to break even on the call alone you'd need to win roughly **7.5×** your call when you hit — but you *don't* always get paid, and you sometimes lose even with a set. So the practical heuristic is the **"10-to-15× rule"**: you want the **effective stacks** (what you can win) to be at least **~10–15× the amount you're calling** preflop, *and* an opponent likely to pay you off.
- **Worked example:** villain opens to 2.5bb. To set-mine profitably you'd like effective stacks of at least ~25–37bb behind (10–15 × 2.5). At 100bb, that's easily met — set-mining is great. At 30bb effective, it's marginal. At 20bb, it's usually a fold (you can't win enough when you hit).

**Set-mining requires three things:**
1. **Deep enough effective stacks** (the 10–15× rule) — enough to win when you hit.
2. **An opponent who'll pay you off** — a calling station or someone with a strong range (a tight UTG raiser who'll stack off an overpair is ideal; a nit who folds everything is not).
3. **Position helps** (you realize your equity better and get paid more reliably), though set-mining works OOP too if the stacks and opponent are right.

> **Why set-mining beats "calling to see a flop."** A small pair has poor raw equity against a raising range and realizes it badly — it can't comfortably continue when overcards come. Its value is almost *entirely* in the **disguised set**: opponents can't see it coming, so they stack off their overpairs and top pairs into your three-of-a-kind. That implied-odds payoff, not the pair's showdown value, is what makes the call profitable. Take away the implied odds (short stacks, nitty opponent) and the call collapses.

## 12.3 Preflop Play by Pair Size

**JJ–TT (medium pairs):** strong enough to **raise and 3-bet for value**, but not premiums. They open from any position, 3-bet against wider opens, and are **opponent-dependent vs. 4-bets** (JJ is often a fold or flat vs. tight 4-bets — it's dominated by QQ+/AK's pairs and flipping at best). Postflop they're frequently overpairs (only one or two overcards can come), so they play more like the Chapter 11 hands than like set-miners — but on broadway-heavy boards they slide into underpair mode.

**99–66 (middle pairs):** the straddle. They **open** and often **3-bet** (more as value-leaning or, for the smaller ones, occasionally as flats/light 3-bets), and they're prime **set-mining flat-calling** hands against opens when the stacks and opponent are right. Postflop they're overpairs on low boards and set-or-give-up on higher boards.

**55–22 (small pairs):** primarily **set-miners**. They open from middle/late position (they're in the opening ranges of Ch. 4), and against opens they **flat to set-mine** when the implied odds are there, or **fold** when they're not. They occasionally serve as **light 3-bet bluffs** (with the plan of flopping a set or taking it down with fold equity) against opponents who fold too much. They're almost never overpairs.

A key flatting-vs-3-betting note: small pairs make **poor value 3-bets** (they're flipping or dominated when called) but **fine flats** (set-mine) and **occasional bluff 3-bets** (fold equity + set potential). Choose based on the opponent: flat to set-mine vs. payers, bluff-3-bet vs. folders, fold vs. nits who won't pay and won't fold.

## 12.4 Postflop: Playing the Two Modes

### When you flop a set (the dream)
You have a disguised monster — now **extract maximum value**:
- **Usually bet/raise to build the pot.** Sets are strong enough to play a big pot, and you want money in while you're a huge favorite. Don't over-slowplay — on **wet boards** especially, bet/raise to charge the draws that can outrun you (a set on 9-8-6 two-tone is strong but not invincible against straight/flush draws).
- **Occasionally slowplay** on very **dry, static boards** where no worse hand will pay a bet and you want to let villain catch up or bluff (e.g., your 22 on K-7-2 — checking can induce more value than betting into a range that whiffed). But default to building the pot.
- **Sets are the canonical "get it all in" hand** — when the money goes in on most boards, your set is happy. The exceptions are obvious scare runouts (four to a straight/flush, board pairing to threaten a higher full house) against tight ranges.

### When you have an overpair (low board)
Play it like a small AA/KK (Chapter 11): **bet for value and protection**, size up on wet boards, but **don't stack off lightly** — JJ on a 7-5-2 board is good, but it's one pair, and heavy aggression or a wet runout can mean you're beaten. Pot-control and fold when the action says so.

### When you're an underpair (overcards on board)
This is the common, less glamorous case. Your pair is now a **weak bluff-catcher** or a fold:
- **As the preflop raiser** who missed (you hold 88 on A-Q-4): your pair is mediocre. A small c-bet can work on the right texture (you have some equity and fold equity), but **don't barrel off** — you're often behind and bloating a pot with a hand that can't stand much heat. Frequently the right play is **check and give up / pot-control**.
- **As the caller** facing a c-bet (you hold 66 on K-J-3): your pair beats villain's bluffs but loses to all their top pairs. It's a **marginal bluff-catcher** — call only when the price and villain's bluff frequency justify it (Chapter 10's bluff-catcher math), and fold to continued aggression. Don't get attached to a small pair as if it were a real made hand.

> **The discipline that defines good pair play.** Small and middle pairs make their money in **two narrow windows**: the times you **flop a set** (extract everything) and the times you have an **overpair on a low board** (value-bet, but carefully). The rest of the time — overcards on board, no set — they're weak hands that should **win or lose small**. Players who bleed with pocket pairs do so by *overplaying the underpair mode* — barreling, hero-calling, and stacking off a pair of sixes as if it were a set. Hit your set or play a small pot.

## 12.5 Stack-Depth and Multiway Notes

- **Stack depth is everything for small pairs.** Deep (100bb+) → set-mining is excellent (huge implied odds). Short (≤30bb) → set-mining dies (can't win enough), and small pairs shift toward fold or, very short, push/fold equity hands. The deeper the stacks, the *more* you want to see flops with small pairs (and the *less* you want to play big pots with bare overpairs).
- **Multiway helps set-miners' payout but hurts overpairs.** A flopped set multiway is still gold (you can get paid by multiple hands), so set-mining retains value in multiway pots — one reason small pairs are fine in loose, multiway games. But **bare overpairs lose value multiway** (more opponents = more likely someone beats your one pair), so JJ/TT on a low board should be played more cautiously with three players in the pot.

## 12.6 The Exploitative Layer

- **vs. stations / players who stack off overpairs:** **set-mine aggressively** (they pay you off in full when you hit) and value-bet your overpairs bigger.
- **vs. nits who fold too much:** **stop set-mining** (they won't pay you when you hit) but **add small pairs as light 3-bet bluffs** (they fold to the 3-bet). The implied odds aren't there, but the fold equity is.
- **vs. aggressive opponents who barrel:** your underpairs become better **bluff-catchers** (they're bluffing enough) — call down a bit lighter; and your overpairs can **induce** (check-call) rather than fast-play into a barreler.
- **vs. fit-or-fold opponents:** c-bet your underpairs more as bluffs on good textures (they fold their misses), but give up when called.
- **Adjust set-mining calls to stack depth and payoff likelihood** — the call is only as good as the implied odds behind it.

> **The leak this chapter fixes.** Two leaks: **(1) set-mining with bad implied odds** (calling small pairs at short stacks or vs. nits who won't pay), and **(2) overplaying underpairs** (treating a pair of sixes on a K-high board like a made hand and barreling/hero-calling). The fix: **set-mine only when the 10–15× rule and a paying opponent are present, slam your sets, value-bet overpairs carefully, and surrender your underpairs cheaply.**

## 12.7 Chapter Summary

- A pocket pair is always in one of two modes: **overpair/made-hand** (all undercards) or **underpair/set-or-give-up** (overcards on board). Smaller pairs are in underpair mode more often.
- **Set-mining math:** you flop a set ~**1 in 8.5 (11.8%)**; you need effective stacks of **~10–15×** your call **and** a paying opponent to justify the flat. Great at 100bb, dead at 20bb.
- **JJ–TT**: value raise/3-bet, opponent-dependent vs. 4-bets, usually overpairs postflop. **99–66**: straddle value and set-mining. **55–22**: set-miners and occasional light 3-bet bluffs; poor value 3-bets.
- **Flopped set:** usually **bet/raise to build the pot** (charge draws on wet boards), slowplay only on dry static boards; the canonical **stack-off** hand.
- **Overpair:** value-bet carefully (small AA/KK logic), don't stack off lightly. **Underpair:** weak bluff-catcher or fold — **win or lose small**, don't overplay it.
- **Deep stacks favor set-mining; short stacks kill it. Multiway helps set payouts but hurts bare overpairs.**
- **Exploit:** set-mine vs. payers and 3-bet-bluff vs. folders; bluff-catch lighter and induce vs. aggressors; always tie set-mining calls to real implied odds.
