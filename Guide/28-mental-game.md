# Chapter 28 — Mental Game & Bankroll

All the strategy in this guide is worthless if you go broke, quit on tilt, or can't execute under pressure. Poker is unique among skill games in that **short-term results are dominated by luck** — you can play perfectly and lose for weeks, or play terribly and win for a session. This reality creates two distinct off-felt skills that separate players who *know* the theory from players who *profit* from it: **bankroll management** (surviving variance financially) and **mental game** (surviving variance psychologically). This chapter covers both, plus the study routine that converts knowledge into skill. It's the least technical chapter and arguably the most important for your actual results.

---

## 28.1 Variance: Why the Math Demands Discipline

A winning poker player has an **edge** — a positive expected value per hand — but that edge plays out over an enormous number of hands, swamped in the short term by **variance** (the natural swings of a high-luck game). Internalize three facts:

- **Your win rate is small relative to the swings.** A strong 6-max cash win rate might be a few big blinds per 100 hands, while normal **downswings** can run tens of buy-ins deep over tens of thousands of hands. Losing stretches that *feel* like proof you're broken are statistically routine.
- **Results over any short sample are noise.** A losing session, day, or week tells you almost nothing about your skill. Only large samples (and honest review of your *decisions*, not outcomes) reveal whether you're winning.
- **You must separate decision quality from results.** A good decision that loses is still a good decision; a bad decision that wins is still bad. Judging yourself by **EV of decisions**, not by **outcomes**, is the foundational mental discipline — and the one that makes bankroll and tilt management possible.

> **The core reframe.** Poker is not a game of winning hands or sessions; it's a game of making **+EV decisions repeatedly** and letting the **law of large numbers** convert your edge into profit over time. Everything in this chapter — bankroll, tilt control, study — exists to keep you **making good decisions and playing enough hands** for your edge to materialize, despite the variance that tries to knock you off course financially and emotionally.

## 28.2 Bankroll Management (BRM)

**Bankroll management** is keeping enough buy-ins in reserve that normal variance can't bust you, so you survive the downswings long enough for your edge to win. It's not optional — even a huge edge with an under-rolled bankroll has a meaningful **risk of ruin** (going broke before your edge pays off).

**Standard guidelines (6-max cash, NLHE):**
- **Conservative / recommended:** **30–50 buy-ins** for your stake (a buy-in = 100bb). More buy-ins = lower risk of ruin and less stress; fewer = faster movement up but higher bust risk.
- **Aggressive (only if you can reload):** ~20–25 buy-ins, acceptable only if you have outside income to rebuild and can handle the variance.
- **Tournaments need far more** (100+ buy-ins) — MTT variance is enormous because most of your buy-ins come back in rare big scores (Ch. 29).

**Moving up and down (the discipline):**
- **Move up** only when you have the buy-ins for the *higher* stake **and** a proven win rate at your current one. Don't take shots you can't afford to lose.
- **Move down** without ego when a downswing erodes your roll below the threshold for your stake. **Dropping down to rebuild is a sign of discipline, not failure** — it's how professionals survive.
- **Keep poker money separate** from life money. Your bankroll is a working tool; don't dip into rent, and don't let winnings inflate your sense of the next session's stakes.

> **BRM is what lets you play your A-game.** Beyond avoiding ruin, proper bankroll management is a **mental-game tool**: when you're playing with money you can afford to lose, within a roll built for the swings, you make **better decisions** — you're not scared of variance, you don't play tight-passive to avoid losing, and a normal downswing doesn't threaten your livelihood or your composure. Under-rolled players play scared, tilt easily, and make -EV decisions driven by fear. **The right roll buys you the freedom to play well.**

## 28.3 Tilt: Recognizing and Controlling It

**Tilt** is letting emotion degrade your decisions — playing worse because you're angry, frustrated, fearful, overconfident, or bored. Tilt is the single biggest leak for most otherwise-competent players: it converts a winning strategy into a losing one in the exact moments variance is testing you. It comes in many forms:

- **Loss/anger tilt** — frustration after bad beats or downswings → loosening up, bluffing too much, "spewing" to win it back. The classic and most destructive.
- **Fear tilt** — playing scared after losses → tightening up, folding too much, missing value and bluffs, playing not-to-lose instead of to-win.
- **Revenge tilt** — fixating on one opponent who beat/needled you → making -EV plays specifically to "get them back."
- **Winner's tilt** — overconfidence after a heater → playing too loose/fancy, taking marginal spots, moving up recklessly.
- **Boredom / entitlement / desperation tilt** — playing hands you shouldn't because you're bored, or chasing losses to "get unstuck" by a deadline.

**Controlling tilt:**
1. **Recognize your triggers and early signs.** Know what tilts *you* (bad beats, a specific player, being stuck a certain amount) and the physical/mental signs you're tilting (faster decisions, a hot feeling, "I'll show him" thoughts). Self-awareness is the prerequisite.
2. **Have a stop-loss and stop-time.** Decide *in advance* to quit after losing X buy-ins or after Y hours, and **honor it**. The decision to stop must be made when calm, not in the moment you're tilting (when you'll always rationalize "one more").
3. **Take breaks.** Step away after a big pot, a bad beat, or when you notice tilt — even a few minutes resets your decision-making. Multi-table fewer when emotional.
4. **Reframe variance.** Internalize that bad beats are the *source* of your profit — weak players keep playing *because* they sometimes win. A world without bad beats is a world without fish. The beat that stings is the reason the game is beatable.
5. **Detach from results, attach to decisions** (Section 28.1). If you played the spot well, it was a success regardless of the river card. This reframe, genuinely internalized, dissolves most tilt at the source.

> **Tilt is a strategy leak, not a personality flaw.** Every -EV decision you make on tilt costs exactly as much as the same decision made stone-cold sober — and tilt makes you make *many* of them, precisely when you're already running bad. The player who **quits a session two buy-ins early to avoid spewing the next five** has made one of the most profitable decisions available, even though it shows up nowhere as a "won hand." Managing tilt isn't soft skills adjacent to poker; it **is** poker — it's protecting your win rate during the emotional moments variance manufactures.

## 28.4 The Study Routine: Turning Knowledge Into Skill

Reading this guide is the beginning, not the end. Knowledge becomes skill through deliberate practice and review:

- **Review your own hands.** The highest-value study is analyzing **your** decisions — especially the big pots and the close spots — away from the table, without results bias. Use a tracker to flag hands; ask "what was villain's range, and was my line the highest-EV one?" (the Ch. 23 process). Tag hands you're unsure about and review them in batches.
- **Study solver outputs for *patterns*, not memorization** (Ch. 25.6). Run common spots, absorb the *logic* (which boards range-bet, when overbets appear, how ranges polarize), and build intuition you can apply to novel spots. Don't try to memorize solutions.
- **Study population tendencies and exploits** (Ch. 26) — for most stakes, *exploitative* study (how the pool deviates and how to punish it) yields more EV than deep GTO study.
- **Work on one leak at a time.** Don't try to fix everything; identify your biggest current leak (over-folding the BB? spewing on tilt? auto-c-betting?), focus on it until it's habit, then move to the next.
- **Play enough volume.** Skill requires repetition, and your edge only materializes over large samples (Section 28.1). Balance volume (to realize your edge and ingrain skills) with study (to grow your edge) — both are necessary; neither alone suffices.
- **Use a structured loop:** play with focus → review the tough spots → identify the leak → study the correct approach → apply it next session → repeat. Consistent, deliberate iteration beats sporadic cramming.

## 28.5 The Exploitative Layer (Of Yourself)

The "opponent" in this chapter is **you** — and you have exploitable leaks too:
- **If you tilt after losses:** institute a strict stop-loss and reduce tables when stuck — exploit your own known trigger by removing the opportunity to spew.
- **If you play scared when under-rolled:** move down until you're comfortably rolled — exploit the fact that you play your best with money you can lose.
- **If you over-study GTO and under-study exploits:** rebalance toward population/exploit study, which pays more at your stake.
- **If you play too much volume and don't review (or vice versa):** rebalance — volume without review stalls growth; review without volume never realizes the edge.
- **If you judge yourself by results:** force a decisions-based review habit (tag hands, evaluate the line not the outcome) to retrain the reflex.

> **The leak this chapter fixes.** The biggest leaks in poker aren't strategic — they're **going broke** (bad bankroll management), **tilting away your edge** (poor emotional control), and **never improving** (no structured study). You can know every range chart in this guide and still lose if you're under-rolled, tilt-prone, and outcome-obsessed. The fix: **roll for the variance (30–50 buy-ins, move down without ego), protect your A-game (recognize tilt, stop-loss, detach from results), and study deliberately (review your decisions, one leak at a time, with enough volume to realize your edge).**

## 28.6 Chapter Summary

- Poker results are **short-term luck, long-term skill**: your edge is small relative to **variance**, so **judge decisions by EV, not outcomes**, and play enough hands for the **law of large numbers** to pay your edge.
- **Bankroll management:** keep **30–50 buy-ins** for cash (more = safer; tournaments need 100+); **move up** only when rolled and winning, **move down without ego** to survive downswings, and keep poker money separate. Proper BRM **buys the freedom to play your A-game.**
- **Tilt** (emotion degrading decisions) is the biggest practical leak — loss/anger, fear, revenge, winner's, and desperation tilt. Control it by **knowing your triggers, setting a stop-loss/stop-time in advance, taking breaks, reframing variance (bad beats are why the game is beatable), and detaching from results.** Tilt is a **strategy leak**, not a character flaw.
- **Study routine:** **review your own hands** (decisions, not results), study **solvers for patterns** and **population tendencies for exploits**, fix **one leak at a time**, and **play enough volume** — in a structured **play → review → identify → study → apply** loop.
- **Exploit your own leaks:** remove tilt opportunities, roll comfortably to avoid scared play, balance study toward exploits and balance volume with review, and retrain yourself to judge **decisions over outcomes**.
